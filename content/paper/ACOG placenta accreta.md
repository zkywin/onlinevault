The document "Placenta Accreta Spectrum" is an obstetrics guideline co-developed by the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists and the Society for Maternal-Fetal Medicine. It outlines the diagnosis, risk factors, and management of placenta accreta spectrum (PAS), a condition characterized by abnormal placental attachment to the uterine wall, encompassing accreta, increta, and percreta.

### Key Points:

1. **Pathophysiology and Risk Factors**:
    
    - PAS arises from defects in the endometrial-myometrial interface, often associated with uterine scarring from prior cesarean deliveries or surgeries.
    - Common risk factors include placenta previa, advanced maternal age, multiparity, and elevated biomarkers (e.g., alpha-fetoprotein).
2. **Incidence**:
    
    - Rates of PAS have increased due to higher cesarean delivery rates. A recent study reports an incidence of 1 in 272 births in the U.S.
3. **Diagnosis**:
    
    - **Ultrasound** is the primary diagnostic tool, with findings like multiple placental lacunae and reduced myometrial thickness.
    - **MRI** may supplement diagnosis, particularly for complex cases, but is not first-line.
4. **Management**:
    
    - Multidisciplinary care is critical, with cesarean hysterectomy being the most accepted treatment. The placenta is typically left in situ to reduce hemorrhage risk.
    - Delivery should occur at 34–35 weeks in a level III or IV care facility with access to advanced surgical expertise and blood transfusion protocols.
    - Conservative or expectant management, such as leaving the placenta in situ, is reserved for select cases and remains investigational.
5. **Postoperative Care**:
    
    - Intensive monitoring is essential due to risks of ongoing hemorrhage and complications like infection, organ damage, or coagulopathies.
    - If unexpected PAS is identified during delivery, stabilizing the patient and transferring to a specialized facility may be necessary.
