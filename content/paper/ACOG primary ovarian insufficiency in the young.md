The ACOG Committee Opinion No. 605 addresses **Primary Ovarian Insufficiency (POI)** in adolescents and young women, which involves the depletion or dysfunction of ovarian follicles leading to cessation of menses before age 40. Key points include:

1. **Etiology**: POI can stem from chromosomal abnormalities (e.g., Turner syndrome), chemotherapy, radiation, autoimmune disorders, or genetic factors like the FMR1 premutation for fragile X. Some cases remain idiopathic.
    
2. **Diagnosis**: Symptoms often include amenorrhea, oligomenorrhea, or polymenorrhea. Testing involves assessing follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH) and estradiol levels, with elevated FSH levels confirming POI. Other evaluations may include karyotyping, adrenal antibody testing, and family history analysis.
    
3. **Management**:
    
    - **Hormonal Therapy**: Young women require hormone replacement to support bone, cardiovascular, and sexual health. Estradiol with cyclic progesterone is recommended to prevent endometrial hyperplasia.
    - **Fertility**: While spontaneous pregnancy can occur in 5–10% of cases, discussions around fertility preservation or assisted reproduction (e.g., IVF with donor eggs) are advised.
4. **Comorbidities**: POI increases risks of bone loss, cardiovascular disease, and endocrine disorders, such as thyroid and adrenal insufficiencies.
    
5. **Counseling**: Emotional support is essential, as POI can affect self-esteem and mental well-being. The term “insufficiency” is preferred over “failure” to acknowledge the possibility of intermittent ovarian function.
    

ACOG recommends annual evaluations and emphasizes sensitive communication with patients and families to address the implications of POI on health and fertility.
