The document, _Gestational Hypertension and Preeclampsia_, outlines comprehensive guidelines for the diagnosis, management, and treatment of hypertensive disorders in pregnancy. Below is an in-depth summary of its key points:

---

### **1. Background**

- Hypertensive disorders, including preeclampsia and gestational hypertension, are leading causes of maternal and perinatal mortality.
- Preeclampsia affects 2–8% of pregnancies globally, with rising incidence in the U.S. due to increased maternal risk factors (e.g., obesity, advanced maternal age).

---

### **2. Definitions and Diagnosis**

- **Gestational Hypertension**: New-onset hypertension (≥140/90 mm Hg) after 20 weeks gestation without proteinuria or severe features.
- **Preeclampsia**:
    - Hypertension with proteinuria (≥300 mg in 24 hours) or:
        - Thrombocytopenia (<100,000/mm³)
        - Elevated liver enzymes (2x normal)
        - Renal insufficiency (serum creatinine >1.1 mg/dL or doubling baseline)
        - Pulmonary edema
        - Neurological symptoms (headache, vision changes)
    - Severe features include systolic BP ≥160 mm Hg, diastolic BP ≥110 mm Hg, or organ dysfunction.

---

### **3. Risk Factors**

- Key risk factors: nulliparity, advanced maternal age, obesity, diabetes, chronic hypertension, multifetal pregnancies, and autoimmune conditions (e.g., lupus).

---

### **4. Pathophysiology**

- Preeclampsia results from abnormal placentation and imbalances in angiogenic factors, leading to maternal endothelial dysfunction, vasospasm, and organ damage.

---

### **5. Complications**

- Maternal: eclampsia, HELLP syndrome, stroke, renal failure, and cardiovascular disease.
- Fetal: growth restriction, preterm birth, placental abruption, and stillbirth.

---

### **6. Prevention**

- **Low-dose Aspirin** (81–150 mg/day): Recommended for high-risk women starting at 12–28 weeks gestation, optimally before 16 weeks.
- Calcium supplementation may benefit populations with low dietary calcium intake.
- Lifestyle or dietary interventions (e.g., vitamin D, folic acid) have insufficient evidence for prevention.

---

### **7. Management**

#### **Delivery Recommendations**:

- **Without Severe Features**:
    - Delivery at 37 weeks.
    - Close monitoring of BP, proteinuria, and symptoms.
- **With Severe Features**:
    - Delivery at ≥34 weeks or earlier if maternal or fetal conditions deteriorate.
    - Corticosteroids for fetal lung maturity if delivery occurs before 34 weeks.

#### **Intrapartum Management**:

- **Seizure Prophylaxis**: Magnesium sulfate is recommended for severe cases.
- **Antihypertensive Therapy**: Acute hypertension managed with labetalol, hydralazine, or nifedipine.

#### **Postpartum Care**:

- Monitor for persistent hypertension and complications, including eclampsia.
- NSAIDs can be safely used for pain management with proper BP monitoring.

---

### **8. Eclampsia**

- Defined as new-onset seizures in a pregnant or postpartum woman.
- **Treatment**:
    - Magnesium sulfate is the first-line treatment.
    - Prompt delivery is recommended after maternal stabilization.
- **Refractory Cases**: May require alternative anticonvulsants or ICU management.

---

### **9. Long-term Implications**

- Women with a history of hypertensive disorders are at increased risk for chronic hypertension and cardiovascular disease.
- Regular postpartum follow-up and risk-factor management are crucial.
